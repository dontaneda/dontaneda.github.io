Job Market Paper

Selective Outreach and Targeted Interest Rate Caps

In credit markets where lenders selectively contact potential borrowers, lending reflects lenders' offer and borrowers' acceptance decisions. By lowering prices, an interest rate cap can increase acceptance while reducing lenders’ incentive to extend offers. When the cap targets a specific sector, changes in relative profitability can lead lenders to reallocate offers across sectors. This paper studies these forces using a reform that introduced an interest rate cap on agricultural loans in Ecuador. After the reform, cooperatives’ lending shifted toward the non-agricultural sector while banks’ agricultural lending expanded. I estimate a model of selective outreach to quantify the offer and acceptance responses and the role of targeting. I find that banks and cooperatives reduced offers to agricultural borrowers, outweighing the increase in acceptance and reducing aggregate agricultural lending and welfare. The regulation shifted acceptance toward banks, whose prices fell more due to the cap, allowing their lending to increase despite fewer offers. Targeting magnified the offer contraction, accounting for most of the lending reduction and generating welfare losses in the agricultural sector. The results show that, by influencing the relative strength of the offer and acceptance responses, policy design shapes the cap's ultimate effects.

Targeted Regulation and Noncompliance: Evidence from Interest Rate Caps

with Manasi Kutwal · Draft coming soon

Targeted regulation relies on classification, creating an endogenous and selective noncompliance margin that can undermine the policy objectives. We investigate this in the context of interest rate caps, which trade off credit access and borrower protection. Using a reform that tightened caps on agricultural loans in Ecuador, we document two strategies used to bypass the regulation and that noncompliance disproportionately affects borrowers with a single lending relationship. We develop an empirical model to quantify the distributional effects of noncompliance and its implications for regulation design.

Credit Supply, Crop Choice, and Land Allocation

Because production costs vary across crops, financial constraints can prevent farmers from cultivating the crops with the highest expected profits. This paper studies how credit supply shapes crop choice and land allocation in Ecuador, where differences in crop choices across provinces are not explained by geography and prices alone. Adapting discrete choice methods from the industrial organization literature, I estimate a model in which farmers choose the crop with the highest expected profit, which depends on crop attributes, soil and climate conditions, expected prices and yields, and credit availability. To address the endogeneity of credit, I construct a shift-share instrument that combines lenders’ nationwide agricultural credit supply with each crop-province’s exposure to different banks and cooperatives. Preliminary results suggest that credit availability helps explain crop choices, with effects that depend on soil heterogeneity. I use the model to quantify how credit constraints distort land use and to evaluate policies that expand agricultural lending or prioritize export-oriented crops.

Pre-PhD Publications

El Impacto de la Dolarización Oficial en la Profundización Financiera en Ecuador

[The Impact of Official Dollarization on Financial Deepening in Ecuador]

Cuestiones Económicas, 27(1), 2017 · In Spanish

Official dollarization can promote financial deepening by controlling inflation and encouraging financial reform, but it also eliminates the lender of last resort and requires financial institutions to hold high levels of liquidity. Financial deepening in Ecuador has grown steadily since the mid-2000s, but this growth coincided with a favorable external environment, so it cannot be attributed to dollarization alone. This paper estimates the causal effect of official dollarization on financial deepening in Ecuador using the synthetic control method and interrupted time series analysis. Both approaches suggest that dollarization had no significant effect on financial deepening, whose evolution in Ecuador follows the general trend of other Latin American countries.
